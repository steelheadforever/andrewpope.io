As a kid, I distinctly remember taking apart my Gameboy occasionally just to look at the green PCB and the delicate soldering of chips and wiring inside. I'd put it back together (mostly) and it still worked. Despite not knowing what any of that stuff inside the Gameboy was, it held a deep fascination for me. How did it all work? This childhood interest was pressed into a family role: the IT consultant. I'm pretty sure this happened to most of my generation.

In the summer of 2000 my dad found out about Napster from listening to a talk radio show. I'd been secretly using the site for at least a year on the family computer, but I feigned ignorance when questioned. The next day he went to Best Buy. He bought a CD burner and plopped it down in front of me, age 13. "Can you figure out how to make this work?" A CD burner was a pretty expensive piece of tech for our family, and certainly mom didn't know what he was up to. A few hours of IDE ribbon cable mismatching and software installs later and I had my dad burning smooth jazz mixes.

A few years after the summer of CD burning, it was time to figure out how to get non-iTunes songs on an iPod shuffle. A few years later still, time to learn to flash a custom version of Android on my Motorola Droid. As I think back on it, even then, it was all changing so quickly. The jump from the remaining forms of analog media to digital media unfolded over mere years. How did we get here?

Chris Miller's "Chip War" does an excellent job of telling the story. We're here now because the revolutions that Bob Noyce and Gordon Moore and others predicted in the 60s came true. And (partly) because of this, I've embarked on a mission to learn more about the mystical semiconductors that now surround us. It's really that same curiosity young Andrew had: how do these things work?

The question is, where to start? It makes sense to start at the basics, like the basic logic gates AND, OR, and NOT and build from there. The problem is I've tackled this topic a few times. Once in a community college basic programming course. Once during an Air Force electronics course. It never really stuck though, despite my innate interest in how it all works. Why? Well, in those previous attempts, there wasn't a lot of application. It was academic explanation. But my formative years were immersed in hands-on application.

What if learning this replicated that feeling of tinkering? Of trying to figure it out? That's really the best way to learn. Circuit Box is my attempt at just that: a simple web-based breadboard to play around with basic circuits. Wire up inputs, outputs, gates and watch the logic come to life. Pull gates together into new gates and chips. Build a half-adder and a full-adder. Eventually I want to get to a clock, a CPU, and we'll see where we go from there. As I learn and build this out, I'll post and share how it goes. For now, you can try out Circuit Box here: [andrewpope.io/circuitbox](/circuitbox). I'd love to know what you think, and hopefully you learn something too.
